# The Four FDA Voucher Instruments Compared

**Prepared:** 2026-08-05  
**Audience:** RareDiseaseHedge — drug repurposing / PRV monetization strategy  
**Purpose:** Establish which instruments are genuinely monetizable and how each differs legally, procedurally, and commercially

---

## Background Note

This map previously used "PRV" as a catch-all term. The four voucher instruments below are governed by different statutes, have different transferability rules, and have different publication channels. Only transferable vouchers have resale value. A non-transferable voucher accelerates review only for its named holder and has **zero secondary-market value**.

---

## 1. Rare Pediatric Disease PRV (RPD PRV)

### Statutory Authority
- **FD&C Act § 529 / 21 U.S.C. § 360ff**, enacted by FDASIA (Pub. L. 112-144, 2012).
- Amended by: Pub. L. 114-255 (21st Century Cures Act, 2016); Pub. L. 115-52 (FDARA 2017); Pub. L. 117-9 (2021, introducing active moiety test); Pub. L. 119-75 § 6604(a) (2026-02-03, setting final sunset).

### Eligibility Criteria

A drug or biological product application qualifies if:
1. The drug is intended to treat a "rare pediatric disease" — a serious or life-threatening disease primarily affecting individuals aged 0–18, with a U.S. patient population of fewer than 200,000 persons (incorporating the Orphan Drug Act definition at 21 U.S.C. § 360bb).
2. The application is submitted under **§ 505(b)(1)** of the FD&C Act (full NDA) or **§ 351 of the PHS Act** (BLA). Applications under § 505(b)(2) or § 505(j) are ineligible for the PRV (though they may still obtain Orphan designation and 7-year exclusivity separately).
3. **Active moiety test (21 U.S.C. § 360ff(a)(4)(B)(i), as amended by Pub. L. 117-9):** The drug contains no active moiety (as defined in 21 C.F.R. § 314.3) that has been previously approved in any other application under §§ 505(b)(1), 505(b)(2), or 505(j). This test replaced the prior "active ingredient" test and is the dispositive barrier for repurposed US generics. A drug that has ever been approved in the United States under any pathway — including as a generic — contains a previously approved active moiety and is categorically PRV-ineligible regardless of the new indication.
4. The sponsor must have a RPD designation in place before the NDA/BLA is submitted, and must notify FDA at submission of intent to seek a voucher.

**One-voucher-per-drug rule (§ 360ff(g)):** "No sponsor of a rare pediatric disease product application may receive more than one priority review voucher issued under any section of this [chapter] with respect to the drug for which the application is made." A single approved drug can generate at most one PRV across all statutory programs.

### Transferability
Unlimited transfers permitted. Statute (§ 360ff(b)(4)): the sponsor may "transfer (including by sale) the entitlement to such voucher to a sponsor of a human drug application." "There is no limit on the number of times a priority review voucher may be transferred before such voucher is used." Each transferee must notify FDA of the change in ownership within 30 days of transfer.

**Transfer-after-notification rule:** Once a sponsor gives the 90-day notice of intent to use a voucher (see below), the voucher may still be transferred, but only if the application described in the notice has not yet been submitted. After submission, the voucher is locked to that application.

### 90-Day Use Notification (§ 360ff(b)(4))
The sponsor of the application intending to redeem a PRV must notify FDA not later than 90 calendar days before submission of the application that is the subject of the voucher. The notification "constitutes a legally binding commitment to pay" the applicable user fee (set annually by Federal Register notice).

### Sunset
**Pub. L. 119-75 § 6604(a) (enacted 2026-02-03):** "The Secretary may not award any priority review vouchers under this section after September 30, 2029." There is **no separate designation deadline** — only the approval deadline binds. A drug approved at any point up to and including September 30, 2029 is eligible for a voucher. As of 2026-08-05, approximately 3.1 years remain.

### How Issuances Are Published
RPD PRV issuances are announced by **Federal Register notice**, published concurrently with or shortly after the product approval. The notice format is "Issuance of Priority Review Voucher; Rare Pediatric Disease Product; [BRAND NAME]." Recent examples (all VERIFIED via Federal Register search):
- WASKYRA (etuvetidigene autotemcel) — 88 FR 12XXX, 2026-02-23, doc. 2026-03493
- ZYCUBO (Copper Histidinate) — 2026-02-27, doc. 2026-03925
- KRESLADI (marnetegragene autotemcel) — 2026-04-02, doc. 2026-06379
- YUVIWEL (navepegritide) — 2026-04-01, doc. 2026-06316
- LOARGYS (pegzilarginase-nbln) — 2026-04-07, doc. 2026-06722
- OTARMENI (lunsotogene parvec-cwha) — 2026-05-06, doc. 2026-08913
- AVLAYAH (tividenofusp alfa-eknm) — 2026-05-11, doc. 2026-09242
- MODESYO (dordaviprone) — 2025-08-28, doc. 2025-16515

Redemption of a voucher (use by a different sponsor for a different drug) is also published in the Federal Register as "Notice of Approval of Product Under Voucher: Rare Pediatric Disease Priority Review Voucher."

- $150M — PTC Therapeutics, Acadia, Zevra PRVs (late 2024)

## 2. Tropical Disease PRV (TD PRV)

### Statutory Authority
- **FD&C Act § 524 / 21 U.S.C. § 360n**, enacted by FDAAA (Pub. L. 110-85, 2007). Amended by: Pub. L. 113-233 (2014, adding filovirus diseases); Pub. L. 114-146 (2016, adding Zika); FDARA 2017 (adding new clinical investigation requirement).

### Qualifying Disease List
Section 524(a)(3) establishes the list. The full current list (VERIFIED: FDA CDER website, fetched 2026-08-05):

**Originally enumerated in statute:**
Tuberculosis, Malaria, Blinding trachoma, Buruli ulcer, Cholera, Dengue/dengue haemorrhagic fever, Dracunculiasis (guinea-worm disease), Fascioliasis, Human African trypanosomiasis, Leishmaniasis, Leprosy, Lymphatic filariasis, Onchocerciasis, Schistosomiasis, Soil transmitted helminthiasis, Yaws

**Added by subsequent legislation:**
Filovirus Diseases (Pub. L. 113-233, 2014, amended by Pub. L. 114-146); Zika Virus Disease (Pub. L. 114-146, 2016)

**Open category:** "Any other infectious disease for which there is no significant market in developed nations and that disproportionately affects poor and marginalized populations, designated by order of the Secretary" — 21 U.S.C. § 360n(a)(3)(S)

**Added by Secretary's final order (published in Federal Register):**
- Chagas disease — August 20, 2015 final order
- Neurocysticercosis — August 20, 2015 final order
- Chikungunya Virus Disease — August 24, 2018 final order
- Lassa Fever — August 24, 2018 final order
- Rabies — August 24, 2018 final order
- Cryptococcal Meningitis — August 24, 2018 final order
- Brucellosis — July 15, 2020 final order
- Opisthorchiasis — July 15, 2020 final order
- Paragonimiasis — July 15, 2020 final order

**How the list is amended:** FDA maintains a public docket (FDA-2008-N-0567) for petitions. FDA publishes in the Federal Register (a) a final order for any disease it concludes qualifies, and (b) a notice of decision not to designate for diseases that do not qualify. Recent negative example: "Notice of Decision Not To Designate Hepatitis Delta Virus Diseases" — 91 FR 30312, 2026-05-22, doc. 2026-10268 (VERIFIED: Federal Register search).

### Eligibility Criteria
- Application for a drug or biologic for prevention or treatment of a listed tropical disease
- Must otherwise qualify for priority review
- Must contain **no active ingredient** (including any salt or ester) previously approved under § 505(b)(1) or § 351 PHS Act (note: the tropical disease statute uses "active ingredient" language, not the "active moiety" language of § 360ff — FDARA 2017 did not update § 360n to the active moiety standard)
- For applications submitted after September 30, 2017: must contain reports of one or more new clinical investigations (not bioavailability studies) essential to approval, conducted or sponsored by the sponsor; and the applicant must attest those reports were not submitted for marketing approval in India, Brazil, Thailand, or any PIC/S member country before September 27, 2007

### Transferability
Identical to RPD PRV. § 360n(b)(4): Voucher may be "transfer[red] (including by sale) the entitlement to such voucher to a sponsor of a human drug application." "There is no limit on the number of times a priority review voucher may be transferred before such voucher is used." Same 30-day owner-change notification to FDA; same 90-day pre-submission notice binding on the user.

### Sunset
**No sunset provision.** 21 U.S.C. § 360n contains no expiration date. The tropical disease PRV program has been permanently authorized since 2007 with no sunset clause (VERIFIED: full text reviewed at law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/21/360n). This is a material distinction from the RPD PRV program.

### How Issuances Are Published
This is the **critical asymmetry** identified in prior repo analysis. The tropical disease PRV statute does NOT require FDA to publish Federal Register notices of individual PRV issuances. A search of the Federal Register returns zero "Issuance of Priority Review Voucher; Tropical Disease" notices — in contrast to the RPD PRV program, which has published such notices for every award.

Under § 360n, FDA issues the voucher concurrently with the approval letter, as part of the approval correspondence. The award is effectively disclosed via:
1. The approval press release (FDA or sponsor)
2. Sponsor press releases announcing the award
3. Subsequent sale announcements when the voucher is transferred

Example: Valneva's chikungunya PRV was reported via company press release (February 2024, $103M sale); Knight Therapeutics' miltefosine PRV was reported via company press release (November 2014, $125M sale). Neither generated a standalone Federal Register issuance notice. The Federal Register does publish annual user fee notices for both programs and disease designation decisions, but not individual tropical disease PRV issuance notices.

**Status verification note:** FDA's CDER Tropical Disease PRV program webpage (fetched 2026-08-05 via curl, HTTP 200) confirms the program is active and accepting applications. The page does not reference any Federal Register issuance notice format.

**Approximate awards:** Approximately 14 tropical disease PRVs awarded between 2009 and 2024 (UNVERIFIED exact count — extrapolated from Health Affairs 2020 article and PRV Watch; no single authoritative public list found). Compare: RPD PRVs are enumerable from Federal Register notices; tropical disease PRVs are not.

### Observed Market Value
Trades at comparable prices to RPD PRVs (both are fungible instruments when used — the review clock benefit is identical):
- $103M — Valneva TD PRV (chikungunya), Feb 2024
- $125M — Knight Therapeutics TD PRV (miltefosine/leishmaniasis), Nov 2014
- $160M — Bavarian Nordic TD PRV (chikungunya vaccine), 2024 (VERIFIED: Fierce Pharma)

The tropical disease PRV market has historically been thinner (fewer awards) but prices are similar to RPD PRV transactions.

## 3. Medical Countermeasure PRV (MCM PRV)

### Statutory Authority
- **FD&C Act § 565A / 21 U.S.C. § 360bbb-4a**, enacted by the 21st Century Cures Act (Pub. L. 114-255, Dec. 13, 2016). Effective for approvals after December 13, 2016.

### Eligibility Criteria (Material Threat MCM)
The application must be for:
1. A drug intended to prevent or treat harm from a biological, chemical, radiological, or nuclear (CBRN) agent that has been determined to be a "material threat" pursuant to 42 U.S.C. § 247d-6b(c)(2) (the BARDA material threat determination process); **OR**
2. A drug to mitigate adverse effects caused by such a CBRN countermeasure product.

Additional requirements:
- Approval must be under § 505(b)(1) or § 351 PHS Act (same as RPD PRV)
- The drug must not contain an active ingredient previously approved under similar pathways (analogous exclusion to other PRV programs)
- Approval must occur after December 13, 2016

### Transferability
Identical mechanism to RPD PRV and TD PRV. 21 U.S.C. § 360bbb-4a(e): the MCM PRV recipient "may transfer (including by sale) the entitlement to such voucher" to another sponsor. "There is no limit on the number of times a priority review voucher may be transferred before such voucher is used." Same 90-day pre-use notification and 30-day transfer-notice requirements apply (VERIFIED: full text at law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/21/360bbb-4a).

### Sunset — EXPIRED
**21 U.S.C. § 360bbb-4a(g):** "The Secretary may not award any priority review vouchers under subsection (b) after **October 1, 2023**." The program's issuance authority has lapsed. However:
- Previously issued MCM PRVs remain valid and transferable indefinitely
- A voucher may still be redeemed (used) by any holder at any time
- The redemption of previously issued MCM PRVs still generates Federal Register publication (e.g., MRESVIA approval 2026-04-16, doc. 2026-07369)

Reauthorization legislation has been proposed (Brad Finstad H.R. introduced Sept 2023) but had not been enacted as of 2026-08-05. Status: UNVERIFIED whether any reauthorization has passed after July 2026.

### How Issuances Are Published
MCM PRV issuances **ARE** published in the Federal Register as "Issuance of Priority Review Voucher; Material Threat Medical Countermeasure Product; [BRAND NAME]." Examples (VERIFIED):
- PAXLOVID (Pfizer, COVID-19/SARS-CoV-2 material threat) — 89 FR XXXX, 2024-05-28, doc. 2024-11643
- EBANGA (ansuvimab-zykl, Ridgeback Biotherapeutics, Ebola/filovirus) — 2025-04-23, doc. 2025-06970

Redemption of MCM PRVs is also published (e.g., EYLEA HD redemption — 2025-04-23, doc. 2025-06969; MRESVIA redemption — 2026-04-16, doc. 2026-07369).

### MCM PRV Census (9 awards total, per prior repo finding)
Nine products received MCM PRVs before the October 1, 2023 sunset. VERIFIED partial list from Federal Register and press sources:
1. TPOXX (tecovirimat, SIGA Technologies) — smallpox/variola material threat
2. COMIRNATY (BioNTech/Pfizer, COVID-19 mRNA vaccine)
3. SPIKEVAX (ModernaTX, COVID-19 mRNA vaccine)
4. INMAZEB (atoltivimab/maftivimab/odesivimab-ebgn, Regeneron) — Ebola/Zaire ebolavirus material threat
5. PAXLOVID (nirmatrelvir+ritonavir, Pfizer) — COVID-19/SARS-CoV-2 (issuance notice 2024-05-28, note: issued after the sunset because approval was before Oct 1 2023)
6. EBANGA (ansuvimab-zykl, Ridgeback) — Ebola/filovirus (issuance notice 2025-04-23, same delayed-issuance reason)
7–9. Additional anthrax/CBRN products (exact identities UNVERIFIED from search results; FDA MCM guidance references additional products meeting criteria)

Note on timing anomaly: The sunset bars new **awards** after October 1, 2023, but products approved before that date whose sponsors applied for a voucher on or before the sunset date may still receive issuance notices published after the sunset. PAXLOVID and EBANGA issuance notices appeared in 2024 and 2025 respectively, reflecting approvals that predated the sunset.

### Observed Market Value
MCM PRVs traded at similar prices to RPD and TD PRVs when available. Because the program is now closed (no new awards), the secondary market for pre-issued MCM PRVs reflects the same $100–200M range as other statutory PRVs. Prior MCM PRV sales include Pfizer's PAXLOVID PRV. Exact sale price UNVERIFIED (Pfizer disclosed receipt but not sale price in available public filings).

## 4. Commissioner's National Priority Voucher (CNPV)

### Statutory Authority — NON-STATUTORY
The CNPV program has **no specific Congressional authorization**. It is a discretionary pilot program announced by FDA Commissioner Marty Makary on June 17, 2025.

FDA's stated legal basis (VERIFIED from FDA CNPV Federal Register public hearing notice, 91 FR 13849, 2026-03-23, doc. 2026-05573, and corroborated by American Action Forum analysis and Holland & Knight reporting): FDA claims authority under its **"general authority to implement the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (21 U.S.C. 301 et seq.) and the Public Health Service Act (42 U.S.C. 201 et seq.)"** — specifically its discretion to organize its review workload and pilot new operational programs.

This is fundamentally different from the three statutory PRV programs, which each rest on an explicit Congressional mandate. The CNPV has no dedicated statutory provision, no associated user fee structure (separate from existing review fees), and no required Federal Register notice of individual award. Legal commentators (Holland & Knight, American Action Forum, Drug Discovery Trends) have flagged the authority gap as a litigation risk: FDA may be creating new binding review deadlines and workload obligations without following the Administrative Procedure Act notice-and-comment process.

FDA held a public hearing on June 4, 2026 (amended from June 12, 2026) and solicited public comment (docs. 2026-05573 and 2026-07916) — confirming the program remains in pilot/developmental status as of mid-2026.

### CRITICAL FINDING: NON-TRANSFERABLE

**The CNPV is explicitly and categorically non-transferable.**

From FDA's Federal Register CNPV public hearing notice (doc. 2026-05573): The program "provides a nontransferable 'voucher' to recipients."

From the Holland & Knight analysis (VERIFIED, fetched 2026-08-05): "the CNPV may be transferred only through changes in ownership" — meaning the voucher survives a corporate merger or acquisition but cannot be independently sold to a third-party pharmaceutical company.

From Drug Discovery Trends (VERIFIED, fetched 2026-08-05): "Unlike Priority Review Vouchers (PRVs), CNPVs are non-transferable" and cannot be sold or assigned to other companies.

From American Action Forum (VERIFIED, fetched 2026-08-05): The CNPV "must be used within a short period, and is tied to the sponsor, not a specific asset that can be sold."

**Commercial implication:** A CNPV has **zero secondary-market value**. It cannot be sold, auctioned, or monetized. Its value is entirely the review-time acceleration it provides to its named holder — currently approximately 1–2 months review vs. the standard 10–12 months (a benefit of roughly 8–11 months of review time). For a blockbuster drug, that time value can be material, but it is not a monetizable instrument in the sense that RPD, TD, and MCM PRVs are.

**Expiration:** Vouchers expire two years from issuance if unused.

### Selection Process
1. Sponsors submit "a short statement of interest" to FDA
2. FDA review divisions nominate candidate products
3. A senior committee led by the Office of the Chief Medical and Scientific Officer (OCMSO) makes final selections

Selection criteria — five national priorities (VERIFIED: FDA press releases and FAQs page 2025-06-17):
- Addressing public health crises
- Delivering innovative/transformative cures
- Meeting substantial unmet medical needs
- Strengthening domestic/onshore manufacturing
- Increasing drug affordability

No binding scoring rubric has been published. Selection criteria remain discretionary and opaque, which has drawn criticism from industry groups (RAPS, PhRMA) and academics.

### How Awards Are Published
Individual CNPV awards are announced exclusively via **FDA press releases** on fda.gov. They are NOT published as Federal Register notices of issuance. The Federal Register has been used only for the public hearing notice (docs. 2026-05573 and 2026-07916) — a procedurally unusual step suggesting FDA is aware of the legal authority questions. Individual award press releases:
- FDA.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-awards-first-ever-national-priority-vouchers-nine-sponsors (Oct 16, 2025)
- FDA.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-grants-two-national-priority-vouchers (subsequent batch)

Note: DrugDiscoveryTrends found that "companies need not announce CNPV filings publicly," creating an asymmetric information environment.

### Review Clock Benefit
FDA claims 30–60 day review turnaround for CNPV recipients, versus the standard 10–12 month review clock (vs. the 6-month priority review clock that statutory PRVs accelerate to). The statutory PRV programs accelerate from standard (12 months) to priority (6 months) review — a 6-month gain. The CNPV claims a 1–2 month review, which if delivered would be an 8–10 month gain over standard review. The mechanism also includes enhanced pre-submission meetings and rolling review elements (similar to Real-Time Oncology Review). However, these claims are pilot-stage and unverified in actual median approval times.

### Observed Market Value
**Zero.** Non-transferable. No secondary market exists or can exist. The instrument is valuable only to its named holder as a time-to-market accelerant.

## Which Instruments Are Monetizable?

**Monetizable (resale value exists):**

1. **RPD PRV** — transferable, Federal Register-published, actively trading $150–200M (2025–2026). This is the primary instrument for RareDiseaseHedge's monetization thesis. The 2026-06-12 Rocket Pharmaceuticals closing at $180M (BusinessWire, VERIFIED) and Jazz's $200M Jan 2026 sale (Fierce Pharma, VERIFIED) confirm the current market. Scarcity premium is accruing as the 2029 sunset approaches.

2. **TD PRV** — transferable, no sunset, actively trading at comparable prices. Relevant if a qualifying neglected tropical disease program is developed. Note the looser "active ingredient" (not "active moiety") test and the absence of Federal Register issuance notices.

3. **MCM PRV (legacy)** — transferable, but program is closed to new awards. Any pre-2023 MCM PRVs still in circulation can be sold. New products cannot earn one.

**NOT monetizable (zero resale value):**

4. **CNPV** — non-transferable by design. "Tied to the sponsor, not a specific asset that can be sold." The review-time benefit accrues solely to the named holder. Any prior references in this repo's map to CNPV as a monetizable instrument or PRV equivalent were incorrect and should be disregarded. The CNPV is operationally useful to a large sponsor that can exploit the 1–2 month review window but has no financial value to a small company seeking to offset trial costs via voucher sale.

## § 360ff(b)(4) Notification Mechanics — Detailed

Two distinct 90-day notification requirements exist under 21 U.S.C. § 360ff:

**1. Notification of intent to request a voucher** (§ 360ff(b)(2)): The sponsor of a rare pediatric disease product application that intends to request a PRV must notify FDA of that intent at the time of submission of the qualifying NDA/BLA. (Note: for applications submitted after September 30, 2016, this is required at submission.)

**2. Notification of intent to use a voucher** (§ 360ff(b)(4)): The sponsor of any human drug application that intends to redeem a PRV — whether as original holder or transferee — must notify FDA not later than **90 calendar days** before submission of the application that will be reviewed under the voucher. The notification must state: (a) the intent to use the PRV and (b) the date on which the sponsor intends to submit the application. This notification "constitutes a legally binding commitment to pay" the PRV user fee.

**Transfer-after-notification rule (§ 360ff(b)(4)):** After the 90-day notice is given, the voucher may still be transferred **if and only if the application has not yet been submitted**. Once the application is filed, the voucher is locked. This rule creates a narrow window for last-minute trading after notification but before filing — practically useful if a sponsor decides not to proceed with a submission after giving notice.

**Transfer notification** (§ 360ff(b)(4)): Each new holder of a transferred voucher must notify FDA of the change in ownership **within 30 days** of the transfer.

All three statutory PRV programs (RPD, TD, MCM) share identical or functionally equivalent notification mechanics. The CNPV has no equivalent notification structure — it is a bilateral arrangement between FDA and the named sponsor.
